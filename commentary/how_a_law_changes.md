# How a proposal becomes law

> [!NOTE]
> This file is commentary. It is not law. The binding steps are in the [legislature](../constitution/legislature.md) article.

## A statute

A citizen drafts a change to one statute file, under 800 words, with a sunset at most 8 years out, and names the constitutional sentence that allows it. Fifty co-sponsors, or fewer in a small sub-jurisdiction, ask for review. The text freezes. For 90 days anyone may read the diff. People and their tools may attach explanations of how it collides with other laws. None of those explanations can veto the diff, and none of them become law.

If the proposal does not grow a power, and objections stay under 5 percent of eligible weight, the text merges on the next 15th of a month. A new crime, tax, fee, treaty, or spending program does not take that path. If more people object, or the subject is not allowed on the consent calendar, it waits for a quarterly decision day and needs more yes than no, with at least 20 percent of eligible weight actually voting yes or no. Delegates count only if they cast at least seven days before the close, so a delegator who disagrees can take the weight back and vote directly. A citizen who is content with a delegate does nothing.

On the sunset date an ordinary statute vanishes unless a renewal has itself been passed by an ordinary ballot. Silence does not renew it. Crimes, incarceration rules, the land-tax formula, and the cryptographic specification are debated on the same schedule. A failed renewal does not repeal them. Removing one of those takes the grave ballot.

## A constitutional change

The same draft and the same 90 days apply. Passage needs at least 80 percent of the weight that votes, at least half of all eligible weight participating, and the same text passed on two consecutive quarterly decision days. That is the super-majority. It is supposed to be hard. Scope, rights, and these voting rules sit behind it.

## A local tax

Residents of a territory adopt a charter by the constitutional ballot, counted only among themselves. After that, each resident has one local vote, and the charter may give one vote to other citizens on a general condition such as owning land there. Adding a local tax on an act inside the territory, to fund a local service, is another constitutional ballot of the citizens who hold that vote. The tax does not reach a purchase made outside the town, a wage earned outside it, or anyone's wealth or bank balance. Ending the tax later is an ordinary ballot or even the consent calendar. Neighbors in the next territory can refuse the same tax. Neither group needs the national council to lose.

## What does not change the law

A forum poll, a model-written memo, a council press release, a treaty that was never enacted, an emergency decree, and a judgment. The decree expires. The poll was never law. The judgment binds the parties to the case. The court has no key that merges or deletes a commit. The signature on the enacted commit stays the signature of the tally.

## Precedent and renewal

A judicial reading can be cited later only when a unanimous national panel tied it to one enacted statute, and only until that statute sunsets, is repealed, or is renewed. Renewal ends the citation even when the words are identical. On an enduring statute, a failed renewal keeps the text and still ends the citations for the cycle that closed. Voters who want the reading to survive write it into a renewal that passes. A reading of the constitution itself never binds the next court. Each court reads the constitution. One judge does not overturn a law for anyone but the parties in the room.
