# Land-tax incidence

> [!NOTE]
> This file is commentary. It is not law.

A tax that falls on land value, and on the cost of protecting land, will look harsh on a livelihood that uses a great deal of valuable land. A farm is the usual example.

That cost is not a penalty layered on the farmer for being a farmer. It is the defense cost of that land, made visible. The farmer passes it on in the price of food. Buyers then see the real cost of holding that much defended land, and the farmer has a reason to use less of it, or to use it better.

Taxing the barn, the crop, or the income would hide that signal and punish the improvement. The national formula therefore uses unimproved value. A sub-jurisdiction that wants to tax income or improvements may do so itself, in the open, on acts and property inside its own territory, among people who can leave. It may not tax a purchase made outside, a wage earned outside, a bank balance, or net worth. The nation does not levy those taxes either, and it does not spread the farm's defense cost onto unrelated trades through a tax on labor or sales.

Comparable sales can be thin. The appeal to a citizen panel, and the reset when an arm's-length sale comes in lower, are there because assessors become a political class when "value" is a private opinion.

Across a national border the same tax can favor imported food, because the foreign farm does not pay this nation's land tax. The defense article allows a security tariff or an import control when a statute shows that a foreign power could use that dependency to coerce the nation. It is not a standing farm subsidy, and it does not change the land-tax base. The voters end it by letting it sunset or by refusing the next grave ballot. A court applies the statute's words. It does not remove the control because it doubts the motive.
